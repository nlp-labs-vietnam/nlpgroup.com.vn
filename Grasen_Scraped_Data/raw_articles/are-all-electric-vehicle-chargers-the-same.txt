Are All Electric Vehicle Chargers the Same? Not Quite—Here's Why

When people first encounter EV charging, a natural assumption is that all chargers work the same way. They do not. EV chargers differ significantly in how they deliver power, which vehicles they support, how fast they charge, and what features they offer for commercial deployments.

AC vs DC Charging: The Core Difference

The most fundamental distinction is between AC (alternating current) and DC (direct current) charging.

AC Charging (Level 1 & Level 2):
AC chargers deliver alternating current to the vehicle, and the car's onboard charger converts it to DC to charge the battery. This limits charging speed to the onboard charger's maximum capacity — typically 7kW to 22kW for most passenger EVs.
- Level 1 (120V/1-phase): ~1.4–1.9 kW — very slow, mostly for overnight residential use
- Level 2 (240V/1 or 3-phase): 7kW to 22kW — typical for homes, workplaces, hotels

DC Fast Charging (Level 3):
DC chargers bypass the vehicle's onboard charger and deliver high-voltage DC power directly to the battery. This enables much faster charging: 20kW to 1,500kW depending on the hardware.
- DCFC is used in public highway charging, fleet depots, and commercial stations
- Charge times drop from hours to 20–60 minutes for a passenger EV

Connector Standards

Not all chargers use the same plug:
- CCS1 (Combined Charging System): North America, widely used for DC fast charging
- CCS2 (Combined Charging System): Europe, Asia, most new markets
- CHAdeMO: Originally common for Nissan, Mitsubishi — declining globally
- NACS (North American Charging Standard): Tesla-originated, now adopted by Ford, GM, and many OEMs
- GB/T: Chinese national standard for both AC and DC
- Type 1 (SAE J1772): AC charging in North America
- Type 2 (IEC 62196): AC charging in Europe

Power Levels and Charging Speed

Charger power determines how quickly a vehicle charges:
- 7 kW AC: ~8–10 hours for a 60 kWh battery
- 22 kW AC: ~3 hours for a 60 kWh battery
- 60 kW DC: ~60 minutes for a 60 kWh battery
- 120 kW DC: ~30 minutes for a 60 kWh battery
- 240 kW+ DC: ~15 minutes or less for compatible vehicles

Vehicle compatibility matters: even a 350kW charger will only charge at the vehicle's maximum accepted rate.

Commercial Charging Features

For business deployments, not all chargers are created equal. Key differentiators include:
- OCPP compliance: Enables integration with third-party CSMS platforms
- RFID/App authentication: Manages user access and billing
- Dynamic Load Management: Shares power across multiple chargers on a shared circuit
- Payment integration: Credit cards, mobile payments, QR codes
- Advertising screens: Revenue opportunities at high-dwell commercial sites
- Warranty and support: Hardware reliability over 5+ years of operation

Conclusion

All EV chargers are not the same. Choosing the right charger depends on the site type, power availability, target vehicle types, user management needs, and business model. Understanding AC vs DC, connector standards, and commercial-grade features is essential for any EV infrastructure deployment.
